Abstract Simulating groundwater flow through fractured rock requires resolving thousands of fractures whose geometry is known only statistically. Discrete fracture network solvers are accurate but slow, which limits their use in uncertainty studies that need many runs. We present a graph neural network surrogate that treats each fracture as a node and each intersection as an edge, and learns to predict hydraulic head from fracture geometry and boundary conditions. Discrete fracture network solvers are accurate but slow, which limits their use in uncertainty studies that need many runs. We present a graph neural network surrogate that treats each fracture as a node and each intersection as an edge, and learns to predict hydraulic head from fracture geometry and boundary conditions. Trained on 12,000 synthetic networks and validated against pumping tests at two field sites, the surrogate reproduces solver results with a median relative error of 1.8% while running about 400 times faster. A deep ensemble estimates the surrogate's own uncertainty, and its 90% intervals cover 93% of field observations.

This paper extends our earlier results [9] in three ways. We test the surrogate against pumping tests at two field sites, we add an estimate of its uncertainty, and we study how its error grows with the size of the network. Sections 2 and 3.1 repeat the method and the synthetic results of [9] so that the paper can be read on its own; Sections 2.2, 3.2 and 4 are new.
2. Method We represent a fracture network as a graph G = (V, E), where each node is a fracture and each edge joins two fractures that intersect. Node features are the fracture's area, aperture and orientation; edge features are the length of the intersection and the distance between fracture centres. A message-passing network with six layers maps these features to the hydraulic head on each fracture.2. Method We represent a fracture network as a graph G = (V, E), where each node is a fracture and each edge joins two fractures that intersect. Node features are the fracture's area, aperture and orientation; edge features are the length of the intersection and the distance between fracture centres. A message-passing network with six layers maps these features to the hydraulic head on each fracture.

2.2 Uncertainty We train an ensemble of five networks from different random initialisations and take the spread of their predictions as an estimate of uncertainty [10]. Each member sees the same training data in a different order. At prediction time the ensemble mean is the estimate and the ensemble standard deviation is its uncertainty. We report 90% intervals as the mean plus or minus 1.645 standard deviations, and we check their coverage on both the synthetic test set and the field data.
3. Results On a held-out set of 1,500 networks, the surrogate's median relative error in hydraulic head is 2.1%, and its error exceeds 10% on fewer than 3% of fractures. Errors concentrate on fractures with a single intersection, where little information reaches the node.3. Results 3.1 Synthetic networks On a held-out set of 1,500 networks, the surrogate's median relative error in hydraulic head is 1.8%, and its error exceeds 10% on fewer than 3% of fractures. Errors concentrate on fractures with a single intersection, where little information reaches the node.
3.2 Field validation We compare the surrogate with pumping tests at two sites in fractured granite, Hallby and Korsnäs, where fracture statistics come from borehole logs and outcrop mapping. At Hallby the surrogate's predicted drawdown is within 0.4 m of the observed drawdown in 11 of 12 observation wells. At Korsnäs, where the fracture data are sparser, it is within 0.4 m in 7 of 10 wells. The ensemble's 90% intervals contain 93% of the observations across both sites.
4. Discussion Matched elsewhere: The surrogate assumes that fractures are planar and that flow obeys the cubic law. Neither holds for rough or partly filled fractures, and both limits apply to the solver used for training as well. Errors also grow with the size of the network: the median error rises from 1.8% for networks of 500 fractures to 3.4% for networks of 5,000, because information has to pass through more of the graph than six layers can reach.
4. Conclusion Graph neural surrogates make DFN uncertainty studies affordable: a study that took a week of solver time takes under an hour.5. Conclusion Graph neural surrogates make DFN uncertainty studies affordable: a study that took a week of solver time takes under an hour. Field validation at two sites shows that this speed does not come at the cost of accuracy on real fracture networks, and the ensemble shows when a prediction should not be trusted.

About overlap reports

An overlap report compares the words of two papers or submissions, including their references. With more than two IDs, it compares every pair.

The percentages

Each percentage is the number of matching words divided by the number of words in that paper. When the two papers are of very different lengths, the two percentages are very different: a short paper can be almost entirely inside a long one.

The references count too, so a percentage can be higher than the overlap that matters. Read the text to rule out overlap in the references and in standard phrases.

  • Matched elsewhere: the words are in the other paper, at a different place.
  • Not matched: the words are only in this paper.
